Governing thought Binding constraint = aggregation + reliable regional offtake

Zambia already has meaningful installed processing capacity and corridor proximity advantages, but the system under-performs because beans are not aggregated reliably and the co-product (soy meal / cake) does not clear into regional demand sinks at predictable terms. The result is ~50% utilization (to validate) and a structural unit-cost penalty.

Political economy analysis implication

Reliability is itself an investable outcome. Export bans / discretionary controls during shocks destroy offtake confidence; buyers discount Zambian supply unless corridor pilots create credible “rules of the game” and reduce border rent extraction.

System physics: why soy is a joint-product trade case

Soy crushing is not “an oil project.” It is a joint-product system: every ton of beans produces oil and meal/cake. If meal cannot be sold reliably, crushers cannot run at high utilization even if oil demand exists.

From 1 ton soybeansTypical outputWhy it matters
Oil~18–20% (industry); older mechanical can be lowerOil recovery is a direct lever on margins; upgrades pay back if supply is stable.
Meal / cake~78–80%This is the volume you must place regionally (e.g., Malawi, DRC, Angola, Zim). Meal clearing is the real constraint.
Losses~1–2%Moisture, impurities, and handling losses rise without aggregation + drying.

What “debottlenecking” means (plain English)

Debottlenecking means removing the specific step in an existing plant that caps throughput or recovery. It is not building a new factory. It’s targeted brownfield fixes such as:

• Replacing/adding pre-cleaners and dryers so beans meet spec
• Upgrading expellers/solvent extraction modules to lift oil recovery
• Adding filtration/degumming to reduce rework and losses
• Fixing energy reliability (e.g., captive solar + storage) so the plant runs consistently

Why investors like it

Lower capex per incremental ton + faster commissioning + uses existing permits, workforce and market access.

The core “lost value” today

1) Beans don’t arrive in consistent quality/volume → crushers idle
2) Idle crushers → higher unit processing costs → uncompetitive oil
3) Meal must be sold regionally; without predictable corridor execution, buyers stay with seaborne suppliers despite longer lead times.

So what?

The investment thesis is to turn Zambia into a reliable meal corridor (Malawi/DRC first), which then “pulls” crusher utilization up and makes oil competitive.

5 brick-level projects Each fixes a trade constraint

Projects are defined at “bricks & paint” level: what is built, where it sits in the chain, and which trade friction it removes.

#Project (brick-level)What gets builtBinding constraint solvedTrade outcome unlocked
1 Aggregation + drying hubs (6 nodes)
must-have		 6 hubs in the production belts feeding major crushers: cleaning + mechanical drying + grading + 20–50kt silo storage + weighbridge + QC lab. Inconsistent supply & quality; no exportable lots; lack of working capital collateral. Monthly bean supply plans → predictable crusher runs → contractable meal exports to Malawi/DRC.
2 “Debottleneck” 2–3 industrial crushers
high ROI		 Brownfield upgrades: pre-clean + conditioning; expeller/solvent modules; filtration/degumming; instrumentation; spare parts; energy reliability package. Low oil recovery (~14% in some ops) and unstable throughput. Lift recovery toward ~18% and raise utilization → lowers $/t → improves competitiveness vs seaborne imports.
3 Meal export “anchor offtake” with Malawi feed mills A structured offtake program: 2–3 year contracts; standardized spec; monthly delivery schedule; penalties/bonuses; shared QC protocol. Co-product trap: meal must clear; relationship switching cost. Guaranteed sink for surplus meal → crushers can run at 70–90% → oil becomes competitive by scale.
4 Border “green lane” for soy meal + beans Trusted trader program + digital pre-clearance + dedicated inspection bay at Zambia–Malawi and/or Kasumbalesa nodes; KPI-based performance monitoring. Delay volatility + rent extraction undermines reliability premium. Cut variance, not just averages → reduces risk premium → makes buyers willing to switch supply chains.
5 Trade finance facility for cross-border meal Inventory finance + receivables discounting + partial risk guarantee tied to warehouse receipts and offtake contracts. Working capital gap (beans + meal pipeline); inability to fund 60–90 day cash cycle. Enables continuous shipments → turns “opportunistic trade” into a corridor.
When to abandon (explicit)

Abandon Zambia Soya as a corridor VC if (a) Malawi/DRC meal buyers will not sign anchor contracts even with price parity, (b) border delay variance cannot be reduced without political sponsorship, or (c) export restrictions remain unpredictable during shocks.

Important nuance

“200 plants” is not the same as “200 investable counterparties.” The investable plays are concentrated in a smaller set of industrial operators plus a few aggregation/logistics financiers. Fieldwork must segment the processing landscape (expellers vs industrial) and prioritize the bankable 10–15.

Site-visit tests: questions that decide investability

These are written to validate (or kill) the thesis quickly.

A. Crushers (ETG/Parrogate, Alliance, Mount Meru, Zamanita)

1) What is the actual average utilization in the last 24 months? What drives downtime: beans, power, maintenance, or demand?
2) What oil recovery (%) are you achieving today by line? What capex is needed to move toward ~18%?
3) What minimum monthly meal offtake (tons) makes you comfortable running at 80–90%?
4) What delivered price gap vs seaborne imports makes Malawi/DRC switching viable?
5) Would you co-invest in aggregation hubs if they are tied to forward contracts?

B. Malawi demand sink (feed mills / integrators)

1) Current annual soy meal demand and import share (by supplier origin).
2) Landed cost breakdown today (port charges, inland freight, financing terms).
3) What “reliability spec” is required? (min protein %, max moisture, mycotoxin protocol).
4) Payment terms: LC vs prepay vs open account — what is feasible with Zambian suppliers?
5) If lead time falls from 30–45 days (seaborne) to 5–10 days (Zambia), what price premium would you accept?

C. Border & corridor operators

1) What causes variance: inspections, staffing, rent extraction, or systems downtime?
2) What would a trusted-trader “green lane” require in practice (SPS, customs, standards)?
3) Where exactly are the queues forming (GPS points) and what is the avoidable time?
4) Which agency must sponsor change for it to stick (political economy analysis)?

D. Finance (banks/DFIs)

1) Can we fund a 60–90 day cross-border cash cycle at acceptable rates?
2) Are warehouse receipts accepted as collateral? Under what conditions?
3) What partial-risk instruments would unlock private balance sheets (guarantees, first-loss)?
4) What triggers would cause financing to be withdrawn (policy bans, FX rules)?
